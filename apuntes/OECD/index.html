Classical model

Potential conflicts of interest are defined as the possibility that public officials’ private interests may adversely influence decisions that should serve the public interest (Fung and Thompson, 2024). This traditional definition clearly entails identifying three fundamental elements: private interests, the possibility of influence, and the public interest. The classical approach has focused primarily on the first two. On the one hand, it seeks to identify private interests through declarations of interests; on the other, it seeks to identify situations involving possible influence through conflict-of-interest declarations that serve a preventive function.2 The third element, the public interest, has remained relatively peripheral to discussions of conflicts of interest, since it is assumed to be determined by the responsibilities, powers, and decisions inherent in public office.

Furthermore, potential conflicts of interest are associated with relatively objective standard risks arising from situations that undermine public trust.3 This point is important because the traditional approach establishes a very direct relationship between the powers associated with public office and potential conflicts of interest. However, the public may distrust situations involving potential conflicts of interest among officials who have no direct authority over the matters concerned.4 Accordingly, consistent with certain previous contributions (Dassen, Duque, and Basch, 2026), we argue that the identification of potential conflicts of interest should extend beyond direct functional connections to encompass potential influence arising from relationships within the governmental structure and objective standard risks that may undermine public trust.

Finally, it is important to note that although potential conflicts of interest arise from the identification of an objective standard risk, the attribution of individual responsibility and the determination of the severity of any sanctions for failing to disclose such conflicts depend on elements of subjective responsibility (intent, negligence, repeated conduct, among others). Nevertheless, it is the latter that determines the prevention of the former. Given the responsibility assigned to them, public officials will seek to identify potential conflicts of interest reasonably and disclose them. Clearly, without responsibility, there is no disclosure.

New challenges

Two recent academic studies address the relationship between AI-mediated public decisions and emerging conflicts of interest: Sanchez-Graells (2026) and Tillipman (2026). From our perspective, we identify the following three challenges to the classical model of conflicts of interest.

  1. Shifting priorities. In the context of artificial intelligence, the emphasis should no longer fall exclusively on identifying public officials’ private interests or their potential influence on public decisions. Identifying the processes, patterns, and outcomes of public decision-making itself becomes particularly important when decisions are automated through AI systems. AI-mediated decisions embody orientations and biases stemming from their training processes. We therefore face a new situation in which interests themselves are embedded by design in large language models.
  2. Dilution of responsibility. As public decisions are increasingly made through automated systems, identifying the responsible authorities and their direct or influential connection to decision-making processes becomes more difficult. Even in the most direct processes of procuring or supervising AI systems, responsibility for AI processes and outcomes is difficult to assign to individual officials. A public official may have had no intent with respect to a decision made by an algorithm, yet may not necessarily have acted negligently either. This is because artificial intelligence systems can produce decisions through automated processes whose operation is not fully known or comprehensible to the official.
  3. Reduced incentives. The possibility of attributing subjective responsibility on the basis of intent or negligence therefore provides an incentive to identify and disclose conflicts of interest in advance. If an official believes that they will not be held individually accountable for decisions made through AI, they will not identify or disclose conflicts of interest associated with those decisions. Moreover, if officials do not assume subjective responsibility for automated decisions, they also lack incentives to identify, measure, and evaluate the processes, decision-making patterns, and outcomes of AI systems themselves.

These issues represent potential challenges for countries, and exploring them requires further research. This brief paper presents them only as possible problems requiring academic and governmental attention.

OECD indicators

Against this background, we conduct a cross-country comparative analysis using the relevant OECD indicators for 2025. The analysis uses a matrix to describe the relationship between conflict-of-interest prevention systems and AI accountability systems. The results are then weighted according to the intensity of AI use in each country. The research relates institutional dimensions such as regulatory frameworks and the existence of mechanisms that are put into practice. For the AI accountability variables, data on effective implementation are not available. We posit four possible scenarios.

  1. Positive extreme. Countries with strong regulatory systems and effective use of preventive conflict-of-interest mechanisms, together with strong AI accountability systems. These countries have the conditions necessary to connect both systems and prevent AI-mediated conflicts of interest.
  2. Negative extreme. Countries with weaknesses in the regulation and implementation of preventive conflict-of-interest mechanisms and low levels of accountability for AI systems used in public administration. The highest-risk cases occur in countries with high levels of AI use.
  3. Classical model. Cases with strong conflict-of-interest prevention systems but no AI accountability. This generally reflects a traditional conflict-of-interest framework that fails to recognize the challenges posed by AI-mediated decisions.
  4. Technological control model. Countries with weak conflict-of-interest prevention systems but strong AI accountability systems. Technology-centered approaches overlook the connection between information about AI and integrity institutions. They enable AI controls, but not through the responsibility of public officials themselves.

To identify conflict-of-interest prevention systems, we adapted the indicator “Regulatory framework for transparency in lobbying, conflict-of-interest, and political finance”, using only the variables relating to conflicts of interest. We also used the indicator “Use of conflict-of-interest prevention mechanisms for senior officials.” Both indicators form part of the OECD Public Integrity Indicators. To identify AI accountability systems, we used data from Digital Government Outlook 2026: From Foundations to Transformational Impact (2026). Three variables were used: “Internal controls in place to ensure trustworthy AI”, “Countries’ oversight and advisory bodies for AI in government”, and “Use of AI in the public sector, by function.” The last variable is crucial for weighting countries’ risks.

For the comparative analysis, a four-quadrant institutional matrix was constructed from two composite indices: conflict-of-interest systems and AI accountability. Each index combines two distinct indicators, weighted equally (50% each). Both indices are normalized as a percentage of the maximum possible score, on a scale from 0 to 100. An absolute cutoff of 50 points was established for each dimension, classifying values of 50 or above as high and values below 50 as low. To represent the government AI use index graphically, a linear, proportional transformation of index values into bubble areas was applied, with a minimum area of 55 square points and a maximum area of 1,155 square points.

The distribution of countries across the four quadrants reveals considerable institutional heterogeneity. Some countries exhibit high levels of development in both dimensions, while others display substantial asymmetries between their conflict-of-interest prevention systems and AI accountability mechanisms. Countries with low levels in both dimensions can also be identified. The intensity of government AI use, represented by bubble size, adds a further dimension that makes it possible to distinguish different levels of institutional exposure among countries with similar characteristics. The results reveal significant institutional contrasts. France and Chile exhibit high levels of AI accountability and robust conflict-of-interest prevention systems. The United Kingdom and Norway have relatively high levels of AI accountability, but their conflict-of-interest systems fall below the established threshold. Argentina has high conflict-of-interest prevention indices and very low levels of AI accountability. However, Latvia and New Zealand also fall within this scenario, but with high levels of AI use. Türkiye and Iceland could represent high-risk cases because they have weak conflict-of-interest prevention and AI accountability systems while also making substantial use of AI in government.

Discussion and conclusions

The results point to a possible institutional disconnect between traditional conflict-of-interest prevention systems and AI accountability mechanisms. The classical and technological control models are particularly relevant, as they reflect asymmetric progress across the two dimensions. These asymmetries are especially significant in light of the three challenges identified: the shift in priorities from identifying private interests to examining the patterns and outcomes of automated decisions; the dilution of individual responsibility; and the potential reduction in incentives to identify and disclose conflicts of interest. Although the indicators used do not allow these three processes to be verified directly, the findings suggest a need to investigate how the two institutional dimensions relate to one another.

The lack of coordination between the two systems poses a central challenge for public integrity policies. Strengthening traditional conflict-of-interest prevention frameworks does not guarantee adequate oversight of AI-mediated public decisions. Accordingly, conflict-of-interest prevention and management models need to be updated by incorporating specific mechanisms for transparency, oversight, and the attribution of responsibility in response to new forms of AI-mediated public decision-making.
